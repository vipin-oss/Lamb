\section{How this booklet is arranged}

The plan you have been given is about a plate made of two materials, one of which is piezoelectric and the
other piezomagnetic, and about what happens to a guided wave when the plate is also allowed to get warm. It
uses words from acoustics, from elasticity, from electricity and magnetism, and from heat transfer. A reader
who has finished school can follow all of it, provided the words are unpacked once, in order.

Sections two to nine unpack the wave. Sections ten and eleven unpack the complex wavenumber, starting from
what a complex number is, because the paper reports a decay and a wavelength in the same symbol and that
should not be allowed to look like magic. Sections twelve to fourteen unpack force in a solid. Sections fifteen
to nineteen unpack the physics that is special to this paper: electric and magnetic fields in a crystal, the
piezoelectric and piezomagnetic effects, the magnetoelectric effect and why it is a property of the composite
rather than of either ingredient, heat with a memory, and the size effect. Section twenty is a table of every
symbol in the plan. Section twenty one is a glossary, and section twenty two is about testing your own work.

You will not find a boundary condition here, nor a solution method, nor a number to compare your answers
against. Those are the parts of the job that belong to you.

\section{Waves in one page}

A wave is a disturbance that moves while the material stays put. Tap one end of a metal railing and your
friend at the other end hears it, though no metal has travelled. What travelled was a pattern of pushes.

Two things about waves surprise people at first. The first is that they carry energy without carrying
material, which is why sound can rattle a window from across a room. The second is that the speed is a
property of the medium and the geometry, not of the loudness. Bang the railing harder and it is louder, not
faster.

A wave has a size, called the amplitude, and a shape. The shapes used in this work are sines, and that is not
a simplification made for convenience: any smooth repeating shape can be split into sines, so understanding
one sine is understanding everything.

A solid can carry two flavours of disturbance. Push and pull along the direction of travel is longitudinal,
like sound in air. Sideways sliding across the direction of travel is transverse, like light. A solid carries
both at once, and in a plate the two are forced to mix at the surfaces, which is where the richness of the
problem comes from.

\section{Frequency and period}

Frequency counts repetitions per second, and its unit is the hertz. A humming transformer hums at twice the
mains frequency, a hundred times a second, and that is why it sounds like a low drone rather than a musical
note.

The period is the time for one repetition, and it is one over the frequency. A hundred hertz means ten
milliseconds per cycle. High frequency goes with short period, always.

Angular frequency is frequency multiplied by two pi, written with omega. One cycle is one full turn and one
turn is two pi radians, so angular frequency is the number of radians of phase per second. Using radians makes
the algebra of oscillations clean, and a paper that uses omega everywhere is using that convenience.

Frequency is not decoration. It sets how fast the material is asked to change, and materials cannot change
infinitely fast. A material that follows a slow push obediently will lag a fast one, and a lag is the point at
which energy starts leaking into heat. Every attenuation mechanism in this paper is some version of that
sentence.

\section{Wavelength and the wavenumber}

Wavelength is the distance between two points doing the same thing at the same moment, measured in metres.
It decides what a wave notices. A wave with a long wavelength walks around a small obstacle; a wave with a
short one is blocked by it.

The wavenumber counts the repetitions in space the way frequency counts them in time. Its symbol is k and its
unit is one over a metre. The useful definition is phase per metre: one full cycle is two pi radians, so the
wavenumber is two pi divided by the wavelength. Keeping the two pi makes a forward travelling wave read as a
cosine of k x minus omega t, and every formula in the paper is written in that convention.

For a plate the wavenumber along the plate is the number that matters, because the plate is long in that
direction and thin across it. The wave may choose a wavenumber; the plate then decides which shapes across
the thickness are compatible with it. The paper is about that decision.

The three quantities tie together in one line. A wave advances one wavelength per period, so the speed is
wavelength times frequency.

\section{Phase velocity, group velocity, dispersion}

Phase velocity is the speed of a crest: frequency divided by wavenumber. If it were the same for all
frequencies, waves would be dull.

It is not. Phase velocity usually depends on frequency, and that dependence is dispersion. A prism splitting
white light is dispersion. A distant thunderclap arriving as a rumble rather than a crack is dispersion in
air. In a plate, dispersion is unavoidable, because the zigzag path between the faces depends on wavelength.

The group velocity is the speed of a bundle of waves, and the speed at which energy travels. In a dispersive
medium the two speeds differ, and a short pulse spreads as it travels. The paper reports phase velocity,
because dispersion curves are drawn with it and devices are designed around it.

Dispersion is what makes measurement possible. A measured dispersion curve is a fingerprint of a plate: change
the material, the thickness, or the amount of damage, and the fingerprint moves. That is the practical reason
this kind of theory is worth writing.

\section{A plate, and why it traps a wave}

A plate is a solid thin in one direction and long in the others. Ours has thickness two h, so faces at plus h
and minus h, and it is treated as infinite along the plate, which means long enough that the ends do not
matter.

Send a wave in at an angle. It hits a face; a free face cannot absorb it, so nearly all of it reflects. It
crosses to the other face and reflects again. The wave is trapped, and while trapped it creeps along the
plate.

The right picture is a zigzag. The wave travels diagonally and bounces, and its forward progress is the
projection of that diagonal. The apparent speed along the plate is therefore lower than the speed in the bulk
material, and it is lower still when the zigzag is steep.

Two consequences follow. First, the thickness enters directly, because it sets the round trip length. Second,
the two reflections must agree in phase for the mode to exist. That single condition is what turns a
continuous set of possibilities into a discrete list.

\section{Modes, symmetric and antisymmetric, and cutoff}

Each allowed zigzag is a mode: a definite shape across the thickness with its own dispersion relation. A mode
is a standing pattern through the thickness being carried along the plate.

The modes come in two families because the plate looks the same when turned over. In the symmetric family the
two faces move in and out in opposition and the middle of the plate moves only along the plate, stretching
and squeezing it. In the antisymmetric family the faces move together and the plate bends. The lowest members
of the two families are the extensional and bending modes, and they are the only two that exist at low
frequency.

More modes appear as frequency rises, each at its own cutoff. The cutoff is the lowest frequency at which the
mode can exist; below it the mode is evanescent, meaning it dies away from wherever it was made instead of
travelling. The physical reason is again the zigzag: at low frequency the wavelength is too long to complete a
round trip in step.

A dispersion curve is phase velocity against frequency, one curve per mode. Cutoffs are where the curves
begin. At high frequency every curve flattens towards the speed of a wave on the surface of a half space, the
Rayleigh speed, and that flattening is a useful test of any calculation.

Because the wave cares about the product of frequency and thickness rather than either alone, figures use
frequency times thickness on the horizontal axis, in hertz metres. On that axis, plates of different
thickness and the same material lie on the same curves.

\section{Complex numbers, built from nothing}

A complex number is an ordinary number with one extra rule attached: there is a number whose square is minus
one. Among ordinary numbers no such thing exists, since any real number squared is positive or zero. So we
name one, call it i, and set i squared equal to minus one.

The point of the invention is bookkeeping. A complex number written as a plus b i is a pair of ordinary
numbers, and it can be drawn as a step a to the east and b to the north. Adding two complex numbers adds the
east parts and the north parts separately. The size of the number, called its modulus, is the length of the
step, which is Pythagoras: the square root of a squared plus b squared.

Every complex number can also be described by its size and its angle from east, called its phase, measured in
radians. Multiplication then has a simple meaning: sizes multiply, phases add. That is why complex numbers
suit waves, because a wave has exactly a size and a phase, and travelling through a material turns the phase
and shrinks the size.

One more relation makes the notation practical. The exponential of i times an angle equals a cosine plus i
times a sine, a fact due to Euler. So a wave can be written as a size times an exponential, and the arithmetic
of exponents replaces the trigonometry of waves.

Then derivatives become multiplications. One time derivative multiplies by minus i omega, two time
derivatives by minus omega squared, one space derivative by i k. Symbols like omega squared in the plan are
therefore second time derivatives in disguise. A reader who can see that can read the model equations.

\section{Attenuation, and why the wavenumber is complex}

A wave travelling forward is written as a size times the exponential of i times k x minus omega t. If k is an
ordinary positive number the amplitude never changes and the wave travels forever. Nothing in nature does
that.

So we allow k to be complex, k real plus i k imaginary, and split the exponential. The phase part turns at a
rate set by the real part, so the wavelength is two pi divided by the real part. The size part shrinks at a
rate set by the imaginary part, so the imaginary part is the loss.

The convention in this project is that a positive imaginary part means decay in the positive direction. It is
a choice, like measuring height upwards, but it must be stated, because a reader who assumes the other
convention will read a growth where the author wrote a decay.

Attenuation is quoted as the imaginary part in reciprocal metres, called nepers per metre, or in decibels per
metre. About eight and a half decibels make one neper, the difference arising because decibels use base ten
logarithms and nepers use base e.

The ratio of imaginary to real part is the number that tells you whether a mode is useful. It is the fraction
of amplitude lost per radian of phase, the same as the loss per wavelength. A mode with a tiny ratio travels
far; a mode with a ratio near one dies within a wavelength. When the plan says that an effect may be too small
to see, it usually means that the change in this ratio is below what could be measured.

In a magneto-electro-elastic plate the loss has several sources. Some is thermoelastic, present in every
solid. Some is the work done against the electric and magnetic fields. Some is the leakage of energy into the
surroundings if the faces are not truly free. Our model keeps the first two and excludes the third by
assuming free faces, and the paper must say so.

\section{Stress and strain}

Bend a plastic ruler and let it go. While bent, one face is stretched and the other is compressed, and the
ruler pushes back at your fingers. The stretching, measured as a change of length divided by the original
length, is strain; it is a pure number and usually small. The push back, measured as force per unit area, is
stress; it is measured in pascals, one pascal being one newton per square metre.

Two kinds of stress are worth naming. A normal stress pulls a face apart or pushes it together, as the ruler
does on its two faces. A shear stress slides one layer over the next, as happens when you twist a ruler instead
of bending it. A solid carries both, and a plate under a guided wave does both at every point, because the
zigzag keeps converting one into the other.

Neither is a single number. At a point there are three directions in which a face can point, and on each face
a force can have three components, so nine numbers describe the stress, of which six are independent in a
symmetric material. When the plan writes a symbol with two indices, it is naming one of those components. You
do not have to picture all six; you have to know that the notation is counting them, and that a material
constant with four indices relates a stress to a strain in a chosen pair of directions.

Strain can vary from place to place. The top of the bent ruler is stretched more than its middle. How quickly
the strain changes along a direction is the strain gradient, measured in one over length. In the ordinary
theory of elasticity the strain gradient costs no energy, and in the theory we use it costs a little. That
small cost is what makes a thin plate differ from a scaled drawing of a thick one.

\section{Hooke's law, and where it comes from}

Hang a bag on a spring and the spring stretches. Double the bag and it stretches twice as far, until you
overdo it. That proportionality, for small pushes, is Hooke's law, and almost the whole of linear elasticity is
that sentence with bookkeeping attached.

The bookkeeping is this. A strain in one direction produces stresses in more than one. Pull a strip of rubber
and it also narrows; the ratio of the narrowing to the lengthening is Poisson's ratio, near one half for
rubber, near zero for cork, which is why cork stoppers stay tight in the neck of a bottle.

Written out in full, Hooke's law for a solid is a table relating six stresses to six strains. For a material
that looks the same in every direction the table collapses to two independent numbers, and those two can be
turned into two wave speeds, one for push and pull and a slower one for shear. Every wave speed in this paper
is a square root of a stiffness divided by a density, with extra terms once the electric and magnetic fields
join in.

Our materials are not isotropic. Barium titanate is a tetragonal crystal with a preferred axis; cobalt ferrite
is cubic but magnetostrictive; and the plate is a stack of the two. A stack is not a material at all, it is a
structure, and its effective stiffness depends on how the layers are arranged.

\section{Balance laws and the word divergence}

Three conservation statements do most of the work. Mass is conserved, so what enters a small box either stays
or leaves. Momentum is conserved, so the net force on a box equals the rate of change of its momentum, which
for a solid is mass times acceleration. Energy is conserved, so the work done by stresses and fields goes into
stored energy, kinetic energy, or heat.

All three are written with a divergence, a word that sounds harder than it is. A divergence is a net outflow.
Picture a small box and a flow through it: if more leaves through the faces than enters, the amount inside
falls and the divergence is positive. If more enters, the divergence is negative and the amount inside rises.
Nothing else is meant.

Put a material law into a balance law and a wave equation appears. The stress follows the strain, and the
stress accelerates the material; combine the two and the acceleration is proportional to the curvature of the
displacement. Solutions of that equation are waves whose speed is the square root of stiffness over density.

\section{Electric fields, magnetic fields, and two kinds of potential}

A charge feels a force near other charges, and we describe that by saying the space carries an electric field.
The field is force per unit charge, measured in volts per metre. Its companion, the electric displacement,
counts the charge you supplied rather than the net field, because the atoms of a material stretch into dipoles
and cancel part of what you put in.

A magnetic field is the same idea for moving charge and for magnets. Its unit is the tesla, and its companion,
the magnetic induction, plays the role that the displacement plays for electricity: it counts the field you
impose rather than the total inside the material, because the material itself magnetises.

Both fields have a potential, and the potentials are the quantities we solve for in a plate. For the electric
field the potential is ordinary voltage, energy per unit charge. For the magnetic field the potential is a
magnetic scalar potential, and in a region with no free current it does exactly the job the electric potential
does: its gradient, with a minus sign, is the field. The minus sign is there so that fields point downhill.

Solving for potentials rather than fields is a habit that pays. A potential is a single number at each point,
while a field has three components that are not independent, since field lines cannot simply stop. Working
with the single number removes that constraint automatically. It also means the boundary conditions at the
faces are written in terms of potentials, which is what your plan asks you to choose.

A word about units, because magnetic units confuse everyone. The magnetic induction is measured in tesla. The
magnetic field is measured in amperes per metre, and the two are related by the permeability of the material,
which for most non magnetic materials is close to that of free space. In our composite the permeability is a
measured effective property, and it enters the calculation in the same way the permittivity does.

\section{Piezoelectricity, piezomagnetism, and the magnetoelectric effect}

A piezoelectric material makes a voltage when squeezed and changes shape when a voltage is applied. Barium
titanate does this well, and it is the reason it appears in microphones, in ultrasonic transducers and in
cheap buzzers.

A piezomagnetic material does the same with magnetism. Squeeze cobalt ferrite and its magnetisation changes;
apply a magnetic field and it strains. The effect exists only in materials whose magnetic order allows it, and
cobalt ferrite is the standard example used in composites.

A magnetoelectric material allows a magnetic field to produce an electric polarisation, or an electric field to
produce a magnetisation. Single phases with a useful effect are rare. What works is a composite: put the
piezoelectric and the piezomagnetic phase together so that strain carries the message from one to the other.
The magnetic field strains the ferrite, the strain crosses the interface, and the titanate polarises.

This chain has a consequence that matters for the whole paper. The magnetoelectric coefficient of a composite
is not a material constant the way a density is. It depends on how much of each phase there is, on the shape of
the inclusions, on the quality of the interface, and on temperature. Different ways of averaging the two phases
give different answers, and the spread between those answers is a real uncertainty, not a rounding error.

Two smaller effects belong here as well, because they are the ones our model adds to the usual plate
calculation. Heating a piezoelectric crystal changes its polarisation even with nothing mechanical happening,
and the coefficient is called pyroelectric. Heating a piezomagnetic crystal changes its magnetisation, and the
coefficient is called pyromagnetic. Both run backwards: an applied electric field changes the temperature of
the material, and an applied magnetic field does the same, the electrocaloric and magnetocaloric effects. The
two directions are tied by thermodynamics, so a paper that keeps the pyroelectric term in the electric
displacement while dropping the electrocaloric term in the heat equation is not conserving anything. Our model
keeps both, and that is a point of correctness rather than a discovery.

\section{How two phases are averaged, and why it matters here}

If you mix two materials you need a rule for what the mixture's stiffness, permittivity, permeability and
magnetoelectric coefficient are.

The simplest rule is the rule of mixtures, which takes the volume average of the property. It is exact for a
layered stack when you load it along the layering direction and wrong when you load it across, because the
layers then act in series rather than in parallel.

A better rule for a material with inclusions is the Mori and Tanaka estimate. It accounts for the shape of the
inclusions and for the fact that each inclusion feels the average field of its neighbours rather than the
applied field. For continuous layers it does not improve on the rule of mixtures, and for inclusions it does.

Our plate is a stack, so the two rules differ mainly in the plane, which is precisely the direction in which
the wave travels. That is why both are carried through the paper as a band rather than one being chosen. If
the thermal effect the paper is chasing turns out to be smaller than the difference between the two rules,
then no experiment could tell the effect from the uncertainty, and the reader deserves to see that on the same
figure.

\section{Heat, and heat with a memory}

Heat flows from hot to cold in proportion to the temperature gradient. That is Fourier's law. Combine it with
energy conservation and you get a diffusion equation, in which a point warms when it is colder than its
neighbours on average.

Diffusion has an uncomfortable feature. It allows a temperature change at one place to be felt everywhere at
once, however slightly. For ordinary engineering that is harmless. For a wave at very high frequency it is
not, because the loss then grows without limit as frequency rises. Nature does not do that, and the divergence
warns that the theory is being asked a question outside its range.

The repair is to give the heat flux a memory. The flux relaxes towards the gradient over a relaxation time
instead of responding instantly. One memory gives a wave equation for heat; two give the version introduced by
Moore, Gibson and Thompson in 1994, called the Moore-Gibson-Thompson theory, or MGT.

In words, MGT says that at low frequency nothing has changed and the classical answer returns, while at high
frequency the flux lags, the loss stops rising, and it levels off at a plateau. That plateau is the observable
difference between the classical and MGT descriptions, and it is one of the things the paper must say
something about.

Whether the plateau is reachable depends on thickness. The time for heat to diffuse across a plate grows as
the square of the thickness, so the ratio of the relaxation time to that diffusion time decides whether the
memory matters. Thin plates are the interesting ones, and very thin plates leave the range where a continuum
description of heat is defensible. That double restriction is why one figure in the plan maps thickness
against relaxation time.

There is an extra difficulty in a composite, and it is worth stating early. The ceramic and the ferrite have
very different conductivities and heat capacities, so they have very different relaxation times. There is no
such thing as the relaxation time of the stack; an effective value has to be defined, justified and, where it
matters, swept.

\section{The size effect in words}

At very small thickness a plate stops behaving like a scaled drawing of a thick one. Two ideas describe the
difference, and both are needed because experiments show both.

Nonlocal elasticity lets the stress at a point depend on the strain in a neighbourhood around it. The picture
is a row of springs joined by weak cross springs, so a displacement in one place is felt a little further
along. Nonlocality makes the material softer.

Strain gradient elasticity lets the energy depend on how quickly the strain changes from point to point, as
well as on the strain itself. The picture is a sheet of rubber in which a patch of rapidly changing stretch
costs more energy than a patch of uniform stretch. The gradient makes the material stiffer.

The version used here combines the two, following Lim, Zhang and Reddy. Each idea brings one length, and the
two lengths compete. Both are written as ratios to the thickness, because a length on its own says nothing and
a ratio to the geometry says everything.

The price is the sentence that closes the model section of your plan. A theory containing a second derivative
of the strain raises the order of the equations, and higher order equations need more conditions at the
boundaries. A classical plate needs a short list of conditions at each face; this one needs a longer list, and
which extra conditions to use is not settled. That decision is yours.

\section{What the model assumes}

A model is not a photograph. It is a set of statements that are true enough, in some range, to answer a
question, and the range is part of the model.

Our model assumes a continuum, so no atoms and no domains. It assumes a linear response, so doubling the cause
doubles the effect, which rules out the field dependence that real ferroelectric and ferromagnetic materials
show near saturation. It assumes constant properties at a reference temperature. It assumes ideal interfaces
inside the stack, with no slip and no interface charge. It assumes the effective constants are known well
enough to be useful, which is exactly the assumption the paper tests.

Each assumption is a place where a referee can push, and each deserves a sentence of justification. The
advantage of listing them is that some can be tested: if a conclusion survives when an assumption is relaxed,
the reader can be told so, and if it does not, the reader can be told that too.

\section{Whose sign is it? A note on conventions}

Half the arguments in this literature are about signs, and almost none of them are about physics. Two
conventions cause most of the trouble.

The first is the sign of the field-potential relation. We write the electric field as minus the gradient of
the potential, and the magnetic field as minus the gradient of the magnetic potential. Some papers use plus,
which flips the sign of every coupling term that contains a field and leaves the dispersion unchanged. Nothing
is wrong with either choice; a paper must simply say which one it used and use it consistently.

The second is the sign of the magnetoelectric coefficient. It appears once in the electric displacement and
once in the magnetic induction. If the same constant appears in both with the same sign, the energy is a
function of state; if the signs differ, the energy depends on the path and the material would be a perpetual
motion machine. So the audit of that pair is not pedantry, it is a test of the second law.

A practical rule follows. Write the sign convention in the notation table, once, and then never discuss it
again except where a comparison with another paper demands it. A referee who finds a sign convention stated
early stops worrying about signs and starts reading the physics.

\section{The four fields in one picture}

It helps to see the whole model at once, in words, before the equations.

The temperature rise is the slowest field. It responds to strain through the thermoelastic coupling, to the
electric field through the electrocaloric effect, and to the magnetic field through the magnetocaloric effect,
and it pushes back on all three.

The electric potential responds to strain through piezoelectricity and to the magnetic field through the
magnetoelectric effect. It pushes back on the strain and, through the electrocaloric term, on the temperature.

The magnetic potential responds to strain through piezomagnetism and to the electric field through the same
magnetoelectric coefficient. It pushes back in the same two ways.

The mechanical displacement responds to all three of the others, and pushes back on each of them through the
same constants, in each case with the opposite sign in the conjugate relation. That symmetry of the response
is the reason the model can be written with a handful of constants instead of a different constant for every
arrow.

When the plate carries a wave, all four fields oscillate together with the same wavenumber and frequency, and
the through-thickness shapes arrange themselves so that the faces carry no traction, no free charge and no
free current. The frequencies at which that arrangement is possible are the modes. Two of them exist at every
frequency and the rest appear in turn. The paper's figures are the maps of those frequencies and of the loss
that accompanies them.

\section{Glossary}

\textit{Anisotropy} means properties differ with direction.

\textit{Composite} is a material made of two phases whose combined behaviour is neither.

\textit{Coupling} is any term through which one field influences another.

\textit{Cutoff} is the lowest frequency at which a mode exists.

\textit{Dispersion} is the dependence of phase velocity on frequency.

\textit{Effective constant} is the value a composite appears to have when treated as a single material. It is
not a property of either phase.

\textit{Electrocaloric, magnetocaloric} are the temperature changes produced by applying an electric or
magnetic field.

\textit{Hysteresis} is the dependence of a material's state on its history. Our linear model excludes it.

\textit{Magnetoelectric effect} is a magnetic field producing polarisation, or the reverse.

\textit{Mode} is one of the discrete through-thickness patterns of a plate.

\textit{Permeability} relates magnetic induction to magnetic field in a material; \textit{permittivity} does
the same job for electric displacement and electric field.

\textit{Piezoelectricity, piezomagnetism} are the couplings between strain and electric or magnetic state.

\textit{Product property} is a property of a composite that neither phase possesses on its own.

\textit{Pyroelectric, pyromagnetic} are the changes of polarisation or magnetisation produced by a change of
temperature.

\textit{Relaxation time} is how long a system takes to return to equilibrium after a small push.

\textit{Strain} is change of length per unit length; \textit{stress} is force per unit area.

\section{How to check your own work}

Five generic tests catch most mistakes, and none needs a published number to compare against.

\textit{First, units.} Write the unit of every term in every equation before coding. A term with the wrong
units is usually wrong in sign structure as well. Keep the table and put it in the paper appendix.

\textit{Second, limits.} Every new coupling has a limit in which it disappears. Set the piezoelectric constant
to zero and the electric field must decouple. Set the thermal coupling to zero and the attenuation must fall
to the mechanical value. Set the relaxation time to zero and MGT must return to Fourier. Assemble the reduced
model separately and compare, rather than deleting a line from your own code.

\textit{Third, symmetry.} Turn the plate over. Dispersion curves must be unchanged for each family. If a
symmetric mode comes out with a mixture, either the coupling is real or the assembly has a sign error, and
separating those two possibilities is worth a day.

\textit{Fourth, switch a term off in two ways.} Set a constant to zero, and separately assemble a model in
which the term was never written. The two answers must agree to six decimal places. This tests your code, not
the physics.

\textit{Fifth, compare with a cited source.} Digitise a few points from a published curve and compare the
trend and the order of magnitude. The constants will differ, so identical digits would be suspicious rather
than reassuring. If your curve bends the other way, stop and find out why before writing a sentence about it.

Three habits finish the job. Keep a log of runs so that results can be reproduced. Plot whatever you are
about to quote, because a table hides a shape. And when a result is surprising, obtain it a second way before
believing it.
