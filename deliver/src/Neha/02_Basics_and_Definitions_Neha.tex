\section{Order without repetition}

Most solids we meet are crystals. Their atoms sit on a lattice that repeats, so if you take a small block of
the crystal and slide it by one lattice spacing it looks exactly the same. Quasicrystals do not do this. They
have sharp diffraction spots, which means their atoms are arranged with long-range order, but the spots sit
at spacings that no repeating lattice can produce. The discovery of such a material in 1982 changed the way
chemists and physicists thought about what a solid is allowed to be, and the Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2011
went to Dan Shechtman for it.

The easiest picture is a tiling. Take two shapes of tile, say a fat rhombus and a thin one, and lay them out
so that the pattern never repeats but always follows the same local rules. A well-known example is the
Penrose tiling of the plane, built from two such rhombi. Stand on any tile and look around: the pattern is
ordered, and yet no two places are identical in the way that two places in a brick wall are. Quasicrystals
are the three-dimensional version of that idea, and one-dimensional quasicrystals are the version in which
the aperiodic order runs along a single direction only.

\section{Why a quasicrystal needs two displacements}

In a normal solid, one field is enough to describe deformation. That field is the displacement, the small
movement of each point away from where it was. Engineers call it the phonon displacement, borrowing the name
of the sound quantum, because the waves of that field are sound waves.

In a quasicrystal something else can happen. A block of tiles can rearrange without any of the tiles being
squeezed. They slide over each other, the way a set of books slides along a shelf, and after the slide the
material is locally a different tiling. This rearrangement is described by a second field, the phason
displacement. It is worth saying clearly what it is not. The phason displacement is not a movement of
material in space. A point does not go anywhere. What changes is the way the tiles fit, and the phason field
measures how much the fit has changed. That is why writers speak of phason strain but rarely of phason
velocity in the ordinary sense.

The practical consequence is that a quasicrystal has more elastic constants than a crystal of the same
symmetry. Some of them describe the ordinary elasticity, some the cost of a rearrangement, and some the
coupling between the two. Once the material is deformed, a phonon strain can generate a phason strain, and
the other way round, and that traffic is what makes these materials interesting to a mechanician.

\section{Phasons relax, and sometimes they travel}

The distinguishing feature of the phason field in dynamics is that it is not conserved. A rearrangement can
happen, and then it can undo itself. In the language of mechanics, the phason has friction. If the friction
is large compared with the inertia, a disturbance in the phason field does not travel as a wave; it spreads
and dies, exactly as heat spreads through a metal bar. If the friction is small, the phason field can carry a
wave with a finite speed.

Both behaviours are described by one equation, the telegraph equation, which contains an inertia term and a
friction term. Turn the friction off and you get a wave equation. Turn the inertia off and you get a
diffusion equation. Keep both and the behaviour changes with frequency: at low frequency the disturbance
diffuses, at high frequency it propagates. Which regime you are in depends on how the wave period compares
with the relaxation time.

This is not a mathematical curiosity. A plate has a smallest wavelength it can support, because a
half-wavelength cannot be longer than the plate is thick. So the thickness sets the lowest frequency at
which a phason mode can exist, and by choosing the thickness one can push the phason mode from the diffusing
side of the crossover to the travelling side. That is the reason a plate is a good place to study this.

\section{What one-dimensional hexagonal means}

Real quasicrystals are classified by their symmetry, just as crystals are. A one-dimensional quasicrystal has
periodic order in two directions and aperiodic order in the third. If the periodic plane has six-fold
symmetry, the material is called a one-dimensional hexagonal quasicrystal. It is the simplest case, it is the
one for which the plate literature is largest, and it is the one in which the phason field has a single
independent component along the aperiodic axis.

In our problem the aperiodic axis is the thickness, which is a deliberate choice. It means the phason
displacement is a function of the thickness coordinate, and the plate faces are where the phason field must
be told what to do. That last point is where much of the difficulty sits, because nobody has a handbook of
phason boundary conditions.

\section{Piezoelectric quasicrystals}

A piezoelectric material produces a voltage when squeezed and a strain when a voltage is applied. Some
quasicrystals are piezoelectric, and the coupling works in the same way, with one addition: the electric
field also couples to the phason strain, because a rearrangement of tiles can shift charge. So a
piezoelectric quasicrystal plate has three couplings in play, phonon to phason, phonon to electric field, and
phason to electric field, and a wave that travels in it carries all three fields at once.

This is what makes the dispersion problem worth solving. Each coupling shifts the branches, and the shifts
are not independent, so a measurement of two branches gives more information than two independent
measurements would.

\section{Lamb waves in one paragraph}

A plate is thin compared with its length, so a disturbance inside it bounces between the free faces and ends
up travelling along the plate rather than escaping. These guided waves come in two families, symmetric, in
which the faces move in and out in opposite directions, and antisymmetric, in which they move together and
the plate bends. The phase velocity of a guided wave depends on frequency, and that dependence, drawn as a
graph, is the dispersion curve. A real wave also weakens as it travels, and the weakening is described by
letting the wave number have an imaginary part: the real part fixes the wavelength and the imaginary part
fixes the decay. Attenuation in nepers per metre follows from the ratio of the two parts. For comparing
plates of different thickness one plots against frequency times thickness, because with that product the
curves of different plates fall on top of one another.

\section{The size effect, in two sentences}

Thin plates do not behave like scaled-down thick plates. Two lengths are used to describe the difference, one
that softens the response because the stress at a point depends on the strain around it, and one that
stiffens it because bending a region costs extra energy. Both enter our model as a ratio to the thickness,
and both raise the order of the equations, which is why the faces need more conditions than the classical
ones.

\section{The thermal part, briefly}

Strain changes temperature and temperature changes strain, so a wave in any solid drags a temperature field
along with it, and that field takes energy out of the wave. In the classical description heat diffuses
instantly in the sense that a change anywhere is felt everywhere at once, which is unphysical at high
frequency. The modified theory gives heat a short memory, so at high frequency the heat flow lags and the
attenuation stops growing and levels off. In a quasicrystal plate both the phonon stress and the phason
stress do work against the temperature, so the heat equation has two source terms rather than one, and the
second one is the part of this work that has no published source.
